ABOUT

Sigmund (Tuntematon) Kotopulo was born in a closed military settlement in the north of the N— region. As a boy he was deeply withdrawn, spending long hours wandering forests and abandoned military testing grounds. He showed an interest in art early on, drawing on the walls of the family home in pencil and building strange objects out of whatever came to hand. His parents encouraged this and enrolled him in art school, where he learned the fundamentals of painting and drawing.

After finishing school, fate took him elsewhere: rather than continuing an art education, Sigmund enrolled in a technical university, drawn to engineering and mechanical design. For several years he worked as chief designer at a factory, drafting machines and mechanisms and confronting, first-hand, the unpredictability and error built into the rush of technical progress. That experience later shaped his artistic language — one built on the conflict between the chaos of human culture and the (not always legible) harmony of nature.

His first success came by accident. Walking through an abandoned factory yard, Sigmund cut himself on a rusted iron structure overgrown with ivy. He saw in it a particular expressiveness and decided to turn it into an art object. The experiment worked: the piece caught the attention of collectors and was soon sold at auction. It was a turning point. He left engineering behind, took his first pseudonym — Tuntematon, Finnish for "unknown" — and devoted himself to making work from found objects and materials. His practice gradually gained recognition for its distinctive style and originality. His work has been shown in major galleries across Saint Petersburg, Kaliningrad, Novosibirsk, Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk, and many other cities, as well as internationally.

For years now Sigmund has worked under invented names and pseudonyms, preferring to remain anonymous. It has slowly come to light that several well-known contemporary artists may, in fact, be pseudonyms of a single prolific figure — some critics argue in earnest that a significant share of contemporary Russian art is the work of Tuntematon Kotopulo alone. Under various invented names, he has made and continues to make series of paintings, sculptures, and installations that go on to become cult objects of the country's cultural heritage. In this way, Sigmund-Tuntematon has become a genuine legend of Russian art — a master of secrets and discoveries who continues to reshape how viewers see the familiar, even while remaining in the shadows.

Despite the popularity of his work, Sigmund remains a closed and deeply introverted person. He rarely speaks to the press and avoids public life. His favorite pastime is reading philosophy and psychology, which he says helps him understand the nature of human consciousness more deeply and reflect it in his work. Another passion is traveling to small Russian towns, where he looks for inspiration and material for new projects.
